Why deadlines in custom furniture making keep getting missed

A deadline is rarely missed on delivery day. It is missed two weeks earlier, when one order quietly gets stuck, nobody notices in time, and then the crunch spills over onto everything else. The customer finds out about the missed deadline last, even though the problem arose long before. In this article we go through why this happens and how to spot a stoppage while it can still be fixed.

If you make custom furniture, this is familiar. You agreed delivery in four weeks, everything started as it should, and then somehow you are always late at the end. It is not that the team is not working. It is that a problem is not seen until it is visible to everyone, and by then it is already too late.

A deadline is not missed at the end, but in the middle

Making one custom piece goes through a series of stages. Drawing and measurements, material procurement, panel cutting, edge banding, assembly, finishing, delivery and installation. Each stage depends on the previous one. If an order gets stuck at one, the whole chain behind it stands still.

The problem is that at first such a stoppage does not look like a problem. One order is waiting for a panel that has not arrived, another is waiting for a machine to free up, a third is finished but waiting for the installer. All of that looks like the normal flow of work. Only when the deadline approaches does it become clear that three orders are late at the same time and that they cannot all be caught up. By then, time has been lost that can no longer be made up.

A stoppage does not hurt at the moment it happens. It hurts two weeks later, when it turns into a missed deadline you have to explain to the customer.

Three places where orders most often get stuck

In conversations with custom furniture manufacturers, the same three bottlenecks keep coming up.

1. Waiting for material

The order has entered the plan, but the panel, hardware or glass has not yet arrived. The work formally stands still, and nobody has marked it specially, because it is assumed the material will arrive. When it is a few days late, that order slips backwards unnoticed, and another job fills its place in the plan. In the end it turns out that this very order had the nearest deadline.

2. A crunch at one point

Most of the work passes through one or two key machines or through one person who does the installation. When too many orders pile up at that point at once, a queue forms. Each individual delay is small, but they add up. Without an overview of how many orders are waiting at that point, it is hard to see in advance that congestion is on the way.

3. The order everyone forgets

This is the most dangerous case. The order is somewhere between stages, it is nobody's, and it simply drops out of sight. Nobody pushes it forward because everyone thinks it is with someone else. It is discovered by chance, when the customer calls to ask, and then it turns out the job has been standing still for ten days for no reason.

Why a plan in your head or on paper does not help

Most manufacturers keep the plan in the owner's head or in a notebook. That works while there is little work. When the number of active orders rises to fifteen or twenty, human memory can no longer keep up. A notebook shows what came in, but it does not show where each order is right now and which one is closest to being missed.

The result is that decisions are made by feel, not by the real state of things. The order the customer called about most gets pushed, not the one whose deadline is actually running out. That way the loudest customer gets priority, while the quiet customer with the nearer deadline is neglected.

What changes when every order has its own stage and deadline

The solution is not to work faster. The solution is to see at any moment where each order is and how much time it has left. When every order carries its current stage and its deadline, the order board shows you immediately:

  • Which orders are close to the deadline, so you know what to push today, rather than going by who called last.
  • Where a crunch is building, for example that five orders are waiting at installation, so you can solve it before it grows into a stoppage.
  • Which order has been standing too long in the same stage, so no job drops out of sight.

That is not more work for the team. It is the same work, just visible in one place, so you spot the problem while it can still be fixed, not after the deadline is already missed.

What it looks like in practice
